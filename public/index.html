What's at stake this year.

  1. A new bond is coming in May 2027.

    The District has started planning a bond for the May 2027 ballot. The money from the $691 million 2018 bond runs out in 2027, and voters turned down a $1.08 billion bond in 2024. A committee of about 100 residents meets through January.

    Sources: Community Impact, Frisco ISD

    Where I standI oppose new bond debt. I'll publish a line-by-line review of what the District says it needs and what it can pay for without borrowing.

  2. Your tax rate stayed flat. Your bill didn't.

    The rate stayed at $1.0194 per $100 of value for 2026. The average home's value rose from $551,246 to $565,084, so the average Frisco ISD tax bill went up $141.

    Source: Frisco ISD

    Where I standNo rate increase, and when values climb, I'll push to lower the rate so your bill doesn't rise on its own.

  3. Fewer students, and a school already closed.

    Enrollment has been falling since its 2022–23 peak, because more students graduate than enroll. The District closed Staley Middle School to save money in the 2026–27 budget.

    Source: Frisco ISD

    Where I standFamilies should see the staffing and enrollment numbers, campus by campus, before decisions like that are made, not after.

  4. Teachers got 2%.

    The 2026–27 budget gave every employee a 2% raise, capped at $2,000 for central administrators, and balanced the budget partly by standardizing class loads.

    Source: Frisco ISD

    Where I standMove money from non-instructional roles to classroom teachers, and show the math every year.

  5. Security spending has doubled.

    Budgeted security and monitoring rose from $5.5 million in 2022–23 to $11.2 million in 2026–27. Families deserve to know what that buys.

    Source: District budget workbooks, table below

    Where I standA sworn school resource officer on every campus, and secure entries paid for from audited savings.

  6. Some complaints never reach the Board.

    Families can be told their complaint stops below the Board, so the trustees they elect never hear it.

    Where I standEvery complaint can reach the Board, in open session if the parent asks. Here's the process.

Why I'm running.

I've spent my career reading other people's books, at Toyota, Optiv, Citi, McAfee, and Intel, and now for small businesses here in Frisco. Good organizations make their numbers easy to check and their decisions easy to appeal.

As a Frisco ISD parent, I used the District's grievance process this year. I learned how hard it is for a family to get a straight answer, and how rarely a complaint reaches the people we elect. I'm running to fix that for every family, and to bring an accountant's discipline to a $750 million budget.

I'll tell you what I'd do, what it costs, how you'd know if it worked, and whether a trustee can actually do it.

The plan, in five parts.

  1. Spending you can check

    Taxpayers should be able to follow the money without filing a records request.

    • Build each year's budget from zero, so every administrative expense is justified again rather than carried forward. Board budget vote
    • Hire an independent firm to audit the District's largest vendor contracts, and publish the findings. Board vote
    • Publish an annual staffing and enrollment report showing where every position is assigned and how it changed. Use it to move money from non-instructional roles to classroom teachers. Board policy
    • No tax rate increase, and no voter-approval tax rate election. A flat rate can still raise your bill when appraisals rise, so I'll push to lower the rate when values climb. Board vote
    • I oppose new bond debt. Before any 2027 bond vote, I'll publish my own line-by-line review of what the District says it needs and how much can be paid for without borrowing.

  2. Results you can measure

    A program that works should be able to show it.

    • An annual academic report: reading and math proficiency, advanced course completion, industry certifications, graduation, and what graduates do next, by campus. Board policy
    • Computer science and career courses taught by working professionals wherever state law allows. Superintendent, at Board direction
    • AP world language courses open to middle schoolers from bilingual families. Board policy
    • Review career programs every year against job growth, regional employer demand, and wages, and fund the ones that lead somewhere. Board budget vote

  3. Parents in the room

    Parents shouldn't have to ask twice to see what their child is taught or to be heard when something goes wrong.

    • Weekly lesson plans, readings, and classroom media posted where parents can see them. Board policy
    • Written opt-in consent before any non-academic survey, health screening, or psychological assessment. Board policy
    • When staff separate a child from a parent on campus, same-day notice, a written reason, and prompt review, except where state law requires otherwise. Board policy
    • A grievance process that reaches the Board. Here's how it would work.

  4. Safe campuses, served students

    Safety and special education are where families can least afford a gap.

    • A sworn school resource officer on every campus. Board budget vote
    • Secure single-point entries at older campuses, paid for from audited savings rather than new debt. Board budget vote
    • Weekly progress data on each child's IEP goals, shared with parents. Superintendent, at Board direction
    • Special education spending published by campus, and a hiring path for experienced therapists and behavior technicians. Board policy

  5. Honest operations

    Public money, public staff, and public buildings come with public rules.

    • Annual conflict-of-interest disclosures for District leaders, with recusal from any decision touching a personal interest. Board policy
    • Every club and program is one of two things. Either it's a District program, fully accountable to the Board, or it's an independent organization that leases space at a fair rate and uses no District staff. No hybrids. Board policy
    • Booster clubs and affiliated organizations publish their officers, name them in their bylaws, and carry occurrence-based liability insurance that names the District as an additional insured. Board policy
    • Ask every employee for five ideas to do their job better with AI, pilot the strongest ones with privacy and security review, and publish what each one cost and saved. Superintendent, at Board direction

A complaint should be able to reach the people you elect.

State law now requires every district to offer a grievance process that ends with the Board. Here is the process I'd propose.

  1. 1

    Campus or department

    The parent files online. A person outside the decision being challenged hears it, and answers in writing within ten school days.

  2. 2

    Superintendent

    The Superintendent or a named deputy outside the department reviews the record and answers in writing within ten school days.

  3. 3

    Board of Trustees

    The Board hears the appeal at its next regular meeting, in open session if the parent asks, and decides in writing.

Anyone involved steps aside. No one who made, or is the subject of, the decision under appeal hears it at any level.

The record goes with it. Each level receives everything the level below considered, and the parent gets a copy.

Every category reaches the Board. No type of complaint ends below Level 3.

No retaliation. Filing a grievance can't be held against a parent or a student.

First 100 days.

A single trustee can't act alone, but any trustee can put an item on the agenda. These are the ones I'd ask for first.

  1. A staffing and enrollment reportEvery position, by campus and department, against enrollment.
  2. An inventory of fee-charging programsEvery program that uses District staff or buildings and collects money from families, with its income and costs.
  3. A revised grievance policyThe three-level process above, brought to a Board vote.
  4. An independent vendor reviewThe District's largest contracts, audited and published.
  5. The academic reportA first edition, with the measures listed in the plan.

Straight answers.

Aren't you in a dispute with the District?

Yes. I have appeals pending with the Texas Education Agency and before the Board over how a District swim program advances students and how the District handled my complaints. If I'm elected, I won't take part in any closed session, discussion, or vote on a matter I'm a party to.

I've published the full record, including the parts that don't flatter me, at whatfisddidtome.com.

Will you raise taxes?

No. I won't vote to raise the tax rate, and I won't support a voter-approval tax rate election.

Can one trustee do all of this?

No. It takes four votes to pass anything. Each part of the plan says whether it needs a Board vote, a Board policy, or the Superintendent acting at the Board's direction, so you can judge what's realistic.

Where do the budget numbers come from?

From the District's own budget workbooks for 2022–23 through 2026–27, General Fund appropriations by function. The figures in the model add up to the totals the District published for each year.
